# AI Fundamentals: How AI Works

## Introduction

Picture this: two people sit down with the same AI tool and the same goal — write a cover letter for a summer internship. One of them gets back something generic and kind of embarrassing. The other gets something so sharp they barely need to edit it. Same technology, same price (probably free), and neither one is a programmer.

The difference? One of them understands what's actually happening on the other side of that chat window. Not at a PhD level — you don't need to know linear algebra. But at the level that matters: what is this thing doing with my words, why does it make the choices it makes, and where are the edges where it starts to fall apart?

That's what this lesson is about. We're going to look under the hood — not to geek out over the engineering, but because knowing how the machine works is the single fastest way to get better at using it. Once you understand the rules, everything about AI — the brilliant moments and the baffling ones — starts to make sense.

## Core Concepts

### 1. What LLMs Actually Are

You'll often hear that a Large Language Model "just predicts the next word." That's technically true—but it seriously undersells what's actually going on.

During training, LLMs were fed massive amounts of text — books, websites, code, documents. The goal was dead simple: predict the next word. But to get good at that, something wild happened. The model built up its own internal understanding of language, logic, reasoning, and even math. **Understanding emerged from prediction.**

This pattern — train on something simple, get surprisingly deep understanding — has happened before in AI. DeepMind trained an AI to play the board game Go, and it invented strategies no human had discovered in 2,500 years of play. They trained another system on protein structures (AlphaFold) and it cracked problems that had stumped biologists for decades. OpenAI trained agents to play Dota 2, and they developed teamwork and strategy entirely on their own. In every case, nobody programmed in the knowledge — it emerged from practice.

The same thing happened with language. The mechanism is next-word prediction, but what came out the other side is a system that understands how humans communicate, reason, and respond across a huge range of situations. This is why LLMs can write code, explain physics, and draft emails — not because someone programmed those abilities in, but because they *emerged from learning the patterns of human text*.

#### What's a Token?

We'll use the word **token** a lot in this lesson. A token is the basic unit that an LLM reads and writes—think of it as a chunk of text. Sometimes a token is a whole word ("hello"), sometimes it's part of a word ("un" + "believe" + "able"), and sometimes it's punctuation or a space.

A rough rule of thumb: **1 token ≈ ¾ of a word**, or about 4 characters. So 1,000 tokens is roughly 750 words—about 1.5 pages of text.

Why does this matter? Because everything in an AI conversation—your messages, the AI's responses, any instructions—is measured in tokens. Tokens are the currency of AI.

### 2. Probability vs. Truth

The AI doesn't "know" facts. It knows **patterns** — which words tend to show up near which other words. It generates text that *looks right* based on patterns it picked up during training. Most of the time, that text is also *correct*. But not always — and the dangerous part is that wrong answers look exactly the same as right ones.

This is called **hallucination** — when the AI generates something that sounds confident and plausible but is partially or completely wrong. Here's the classic way to catch it in the wild:

Ask any AI (without web search enabled) to give you 5 sources with links for a research topic. The response will look perfect — real-sounding author names, legit-looking journal titles, properly formatted URLs. Now open those links. Some of them won't go anywhere. The AI didn't find those sources and report them to you — it *generated* text that matches the pattern of what a list of sources looks like. It's the difference between someone who checked the library and someone who's really good at guessing what a bibliography should look like.

- **Hallucinations are not random glitches.** They happen because the AI is always generating the most likely next words. When it doesn't have real information to draw from, "most likely" still produces something that *sounds* authoritative.
- **You can't tell from the tone.** The AI sounds exactly as confident when it's right as when it's wrong. There's no hesitation, no "I'm not sure about this."
- **Rule**: If it matters, verify it. Especially links, quotes, statistics, and citations.

### 3. The Context Window: Your AI's Piece of Paper

Imagine every AI conversation is a **piece of paper**. Every input you give and every output you receive gets written on this paper. Your messages, the AI's responses, any instructions, any documents you paste in—all of it is being written on the same shared page.

The **context window** is the size of that piece of paper.

- It's measured in **tokens** (roughly ¾ of a word). A model might have a context window of 8,000 tokens, 128,000 tokens, or even 1,000,000+ tokens.
- **Everything shares the same page.** Your messages, the AI's replies, system instructions, pasted documents—all of it is written on the same piece of paper, and you only have so much space.
- When the paper fills up, something has to go. Older parts of the conversation get erased from the top to make room at the bottom.

![The Context Window as a piece of paper in three stages: Fresh, Context Rot, and Overflow. See text description below.](/images/lessons/1.1/context-window.webp)

*Three pieces of paper shown side by side representing the lifecycle of an AI conversation. "Fresh": a mostly empty page with a System message ("You are a helpful assistant"), a User message, and an Assistant response — everything is crisp and legible. "Context Rot": the paper is nearly full with alternating User and Assistant messages, but the messages in the middle are blurred and hard to read while the top and bottom remain clear. "Overflow": the paper is completely full — the earliest messages at the top are being torn away and a pencil is actively writing new lines at the bottom, with some text trailing off the page's edge. This shows how conversations start clean, develop attention gaps in the middle, and eventually lose their earliest content entirely.*

Here's where this gets real:

- **Long conversations degrade.** As the paper fills up, there's more writing to re-read and less room for new thinking.
- **Pasting a huge document uses space.** If you paste a 50-page report, you've just filled most of your paper with that document alone.
- **Starting fresh is a strategy, not a failure.** Sometimes the best thing you can do is grab a new piece of paper—a new conversation with a clear, focused prompt—rather than continuing to squeeze into the margins of an old one.

### 4. The Cast of Characters: Message Types

Behind the scenes, every AI conversation is made up of different **message types**, each with a distinct role. Think of it like a play:

| Message Type | Role | Analogy |
|---|---|---|
| **System** | Director's notes | Stage directions the audience never sees. Sets the AI's behavior, personality, and rules. |
| **User** | Your lines | Everything you type. Your questions, instructions, and context. |
| **Assistant** | AI's lines | Everything the AI generates in response. |
| **Tool** | External resources | Results from anything the AI reaches for beyond its own brain — web searches, calculator results, file contents, code output — that get fed back into the conversation. |

![The Cast of Characters — a theater stage showing the four AI message types as roles in a play. See text description below.](/images/lessons/1.1/message-types.webp)

*A theater stage scene illustrating the four AI message types. Stage left, a director wearing headphones stands partially hidden behind the curtain holding a clipboard labeled "System" — he sets the rules but the audience never sees him. Center stage under a spotlight, a person labeled "User" faces a friendly robot labeled "Assistant," mid-conversation. Off to the right, a stagehand labeled "Tool" wheels in a cart loaded with a magnifying glass, calculator, and globe — representing external resources like web searches and calculations that get fed back into the conversation.*

Why does this matter?

- **System messages set the stage.** When a product like ChatGPT says "You are a helpful assistant," that's a system message shaping everything that follows. Custom instructions and "GPTs" work through system messages.
- **The AI treats its own previous responses as context.** If the AI said something wrong three messages ago, it will build on that wrong answer because it's now part of the script. It's like improv's golden rule, *yes...and*.
- **Tool messages are how AI connects to the real world.** When an AI searches the web, runs code, checks a calculator, or reads a file, the result comes back as a tool message. It's the AI equivalent of writing something down, looking something up, or using any resource beyond its own brain.

Understanding these roles helps you see through the interface. **Every** AI chat app is just a nice wrapper around this structured conversation.

#### The Human Simulator: Role, Context, Task

Because LLMs learned from human text, they're essentially **human simulators**. They learned what a likely human response looks like for any given situation. This is the insight that makes prompting finally click.

Think of it like playing **The Sims**—or being a boss delegating work. You're setting up a scenario and the AI generates the most likely response *a person in that scenario would give*. The three ingredients of a good prompt map directly to this:

- **Role**: Who is the AI simulating? ("You are a senior marketing copywriter...")
- **Context**: What's the situation? ("We're launching a new product for small businesses...")
- **Task**: What do you need? ("Write three subject lines for our launch email.")

Change the role and the same task produces completely different output. Ask a "marketing copywriter" vs. a "data analyst" to help with your product launch and you'll get very different responses—because the AI is simulating different people in different contexts. The better you set the scene, the better the performance.

### 5. The Goldfish with a Journal: Statelessness

Here is the most important thing most people get wrong about AI:

> **The AI does not remember you. It does not remember your last conversation. Every single time you send a message, it reads the entire conversation from the very beginning.**

Think of it as a goldfish with a journal. Every time you speak to it, the goldfish:

1. Picks up the journal (your full conversation so far)
2. Reads it from page one
3. Writes its response on the next page
4. Puts the journal down and immediately forgets everything

![The Goldfish with a Journal — a four-panel comic showing how AI processes each message. See text description below.](/images/lessons/1.1/goldfish.webp)

*A four-panel comic strip titled "The Goldfish with a Journal: Statelessness." Panel 1 — "Picks Up Journal": a cartoon goldfish in a bowl reaches for a notebook labeled "Conversation Log." Panel 2 — "Reads Journal": the goldfish, now wearing reading glasses, reads through the journal which shows previous User and Assistant messages. Panel 3 — "Writes Response": the goldfish writes its reply on the next page with a quill pen. Panel 4 — "Forgets Everything": the goldfish is back in its bowl with a completely blank expression and an empty thought bubble, having immediately forgotten everything it just read and wrote.*

When you start a new conversation, it's a brand new journal. The goldfish has no idea who you are or what you talked about yesterday.

#### "But Wait — ChatGPT Remembers Me?"

If you use ChatGPT's "Memory" feature, it feels like the AI knows you across conversations. It remembers your name, your preferences, your job. So doesn't that disprove statelessness?

No — and this is where it gets interesting. Here's what's actually happening behind the scenes: when you enable Memory, ChatGPT saves small notes about you (like "User's name is Alex" or "User prefers concise responses"). Every time you start a new conversation, those notes get **quietly injected into the system message** — the director's notes you never see. The AI isn't *remembering* you. It's *reading about* you at the top of every new journal, like a sticky note on the cover that says "here's what you need to know about this person."

This is true for every "memory" or "personalization" feature in any AI product. There is no actual memory. There's only the context window — your piece of paper — and whatever gets written on it before you start talking.

This means:

- **Every message you send means the AI re-processes the entire conversation**, not just the new message. It re-reads the whole thing, every single time.
- **Conversations get slower and more expensive the longer they go**, because the journal keeps growing.
- **If you want the AI to know something, it must be in the current conversation.** Don't assume it "remembers" from earlier. If it's important, repeat it or put it in the system message.

### 6. Context Rot: Lost in the Middle

When the AI reads your conversation (its "journal"), it doesn't pay equal attention to everything. It turns out LLMs pay the most attention to:

1. **The beginning** of the context (system messages, early instructions)
2. **The end** of the context (your most recent messages)
3. **Less attention to the middle** (the long stretch of conversation in between)

Think of it like scrolling back through a massive group chat or Discord thread. You remember the first few messages that started the conversation. You remember the most recent ones you just read. But everything in the middle? It's a blur—you'd have to specifically scroll back and look for it.

This is called the **"lost in the middle"** problem—and in long conversations, it creates **context rot**. The longer your conversation goes, the more stuff piles up in that low-attention middle zone. Instructions you gave early on start to lose their grip. The AI's responses drift from what you originally asked for — not because it's ignoring you, but because your instructions are buried under pages and pages of back-and-forth.

Practical strategies for fighting context rot:

- **Put your most important instructions at the beginning or end**, not buried in paragraph 4 of a 10-paragraph prompt.
- **If the AI "forgets" something you said earlier**, it might not be gone from the context—it might just be rotting in the middle.
- **Restate key requirements in your latest message.** Even if you said it before, saying it again at the end brings it back to the front of the AI's attention.
- **Save important outputs along the way.** Have the AI write key results, summaries, or decisions to a file (or just copy them yourself). That way, if things start to drift and you need to start fresh, you don't lose what you already got done. You can paste those saved outputs into a new conversation as ready-made context.
- **Start a fresh conversation when things feel off.** A clean piece of paper with a well-crafted prompt—plus your saved outputs—often beats a long, drifting thread.

## Activities

1. **Role Shifting**: Give the AI a specific role, context, and task (e.g., "You are a children's book author. I'm writing a story about a brave turtle. Write the opening paragraph."). Then change *only* the role to "horror novelist" and give the same context and task. Compare how differently it responds to the same scenario with a different simulated person.
2. **Source Check**: Pick any research topic. Ask an AI (with web search turned off) to "give me 5 academic sources with links about [topic]." It'll produce a professional-looking list. Now click every single link. How many actually work? How many lead to real papers by the listed authors? This is hallucination in action — and it's exactly the kind of thing that gets people burned.
3. **Experience Statelessness**: Have a conversation with an AI. Close it. Open a brand new conversation and ask: "What did we just talk about?" Notice how it has zero knowledge of the previous conversation.
4. **Context Rot Test**: In a long conversation (10+ messages back and forth), bury a specific instruction in the middle, like "From now on, end every response with the word 'banana.'" Keep chatting about other topics. See how quickly the instruction rots away and the AI stops following it.
