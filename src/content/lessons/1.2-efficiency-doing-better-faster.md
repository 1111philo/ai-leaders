# Efficiency: Doing Better, Faster

## Introduction

You've got a 47-message group chat about a class project, and your professor wants a one-page summary of what was decided by tomorrow morning. Or maybe you just sat through a 40-minute meeting and now you're staring at two pages of messy handwritten notes that somehow need to become an email with clear action items. Or you need to rewrite your college application essay for a different scholarship — same experiences, completely different framing.

You already know how to do all of these things. You could grind through them. But every one of those tasks has something in common: you're not creating something new. You're reshaping information that already exists. You're changing format, length, structure, or audience — and that kind of work is exactly where AI goes from "interesting toy" to genuine superpower.

Here's where everything you learned in Lesson 1.1 gets practical. You know about tokens, context windows, the goldfish with a journal — the rules of how the machine works. Now the question shifts: "Should I hand this to AI, and if so, how do I get the best result?" Figuring that out is the difference between someone who uses AI and someone who's genuinely efficient with it.

## Core Concepts

### 1. The Sous Chef: What to Delegate

If you've ever watched a cooking competition — MasterChef, Chopped, anything on YouTube — you've seen this dynamic: the head chef isn't doing everything alone. There's a sous chef handling the prep work — chopping ingredients, measuring portions, organizing the station — so the head chef can focus on the creative decisions. The sous chef doesn't decide what goes on the menu. They don't taste-test the final dish. But they make it possible for the chef to do their best work without spending three hours dicing onions.

AI is your sous chef.

The key insight is knowing **what to delegate and what to keep**. Here's the mental model: if a task is mostly about *processing*, *reformatting*, or *organizing* information that already exists, it's sous chef territory. If it requires *original judgment*, *personal voice*, or *high-stakes accuracy*, that's head chef work — yours.

Some tasks are obvious calls. Notice how the "sous chef" side is always about reshaping existing information, while the "head chef" side is always about judgment and originality:

- **Sous chef work**: Summarizing a long article, converting a paragraph into bullet points, extracting dates from an email chain, reformatting data into a table, translating a message into another language
- **Head chef work**: Deciding which points from that article matter most for *your* argument, choosing the right tone for a sensitive email, making a judgment call on ambiguous data, writing something that needs to sound like *you*

The gray area in between is where the real skill develops. Writing a first draft of a college application essay? That's sous chef work — AI can get you a starting structure. But deciding which experiences to highlight and what tone matches the school's culture? That's you. Similarly, AI can draft a thank-you message after a job interview, but knowing *what* to thank them for and *how* personal to get requires your judgment. The best results come from knowing where to draw that line.

### 2. Transformation: Changing Shape, Not Meaning

Think about Google Translate. You paste in an English paragraph, you get back a Spanish paragraph. The *meaning* is the same — the *shape* changed. That's a transformation. AI is phenomenally good at this kind of work, and not just with languages.

**Transformation tasks** are anything where you're taking existing information and changing its format, length, structure, or audience without changing what it actually says. The meaning goes in, the meaning comes out — just wearing different clothes.

Here are the big categories:

- **Summarization**: "Turn this 2,000-word article into 3 bullet points." You're compressing — same meaning, smaller container.
- **Formatting**: "Take this wall of text and organize it into a table with columns for Name, Date, and Action Item." Same information, different structure.
- **Translation**: "Rewrite this technical explanation so a 10-year-old could understand it." You're not just translating between languages — you're translating between *audiences*.
- **Extraction**: "Pull out every date, name, and dollar amount from this contract." The information is already in there — you just need it separated out.

Think of it like remixing a song versus writing an original. A remix takes existing music and reshapes it — new beat, different arrangement, maybe a different mood. But the core melody is still there. That's what AI does with transformation tasks. It's an incredible remixer. But there's a limit to this analogy: a human remixer has artistic intent — they're making creative choices about what sounds good. AI isn't making creative choices. It's recognizing patterns and generating the most likely reshaping. For transformation, that's a strength — you *want* it to preserve meaning, not get creative with it. But ask it to write a hit song from scratch with genuine emotional depth? That's a different kind of work entirely.

The reason AI is so good at transformation goes back to what you learned in Lesson 1.1. LLMs learned the patterns of human language — and transformation is all about patterns. Summarizing follows a pattern. Formatting follows a pattern. Translating between registers follows a pattern. The AI isn't "understanding" your document the way you do. It's recognizing the structural pattern of the input and generating the structural pattern of the output. For transformation work, that's more than enough.

### 3. The 0-to-0.8 Draft

Here's a scenario you've probably lived: you need to write something — an essay, an email, a project proposal — and you spend 45 minutes staring at a blank page. You write a sentence, delete it, write another one, delete that too. The blank page is the enemy. Once you have *something* on the page, even something rough, the work starts flowing. The hard part was never the editing. It was the starting.

AI demolishes the blank page problem.

Think of it like video editing. A rough cut is the first assembly of your footage — you've got all the clips in roughly the right order, the story makes sense, but it's not polished. The color isn't graded, the transitions are choppy, the pacing is off. No editor would ship a rough cut. But no editor starts from nothing, either. The rough cut is the essential raw material that makes the final product possible.

That's what AI gives you: **the 0-to-0.8 draft**. It gets you from a blank page (0) to a solid rough draft (0.8) in minutes instead of hours. But here's the critical part — it's almost never a 1.0. That last 20% is where your judgment, your voice, your expertise, and your knowledge of the specific situation come in.

Why 0.8 and not 1.0? Because of what you learned about probability in Lesson 1.1. The AI generates the *most likely* response, not the *best* response for your specific situation. It doesn't know your professor's pet peeves, your club's inside jokes, or the subtle politics of why you need to phrase something carefully. It gives you the statistically average good answer. You turn it into the *right* answer.

#### Where the 0-to-0.8 Works Best

Not every task benefits equally from the 0-to-0.8 approach. It shines brightest when the hardest part is getting started — when the structure is more important than the specifics:

- **Emails and messages**: AI drafts the structure and tone; you adjust for the specific relationship and context
- **First drafts of essays or reports**: AI builds the skeleton; you add the original thinking and personal voice
- **Outlines and brainstorming**: AI generates a starting framework; you reshape it based on what you actually know
- **Code and technical writing**: AI produces working boilerplate; you customize it for your specific use case

The trap to avoid: treating the 0.8 as a 1.0. If you copy-paste AI output without reading and editing it, you're not being efficient — you're being lazy, and people can tell. The efficiency gain is in skipping the blank-page paralysis, not in skipping the thinking.

### 4. Prompting for Efficiency

Remember the **Role/Context/Task** framework from Lesson 1.1 — the idea that you're setting up a simulation, and the better you set the scene, the better the AI performs? Everything from that lesson still applies. But for efficiency work, there's a fourth ingredient worth adding: **Constraints**.

**Constraints** are the guardrails that keep the output in the shape you actually need. Without them, the AI gives you its best guess at what you want — which might be a 500-word essay when you needed 3 bullet points, or a formal report when you needed a casual Slack message.

Here's what the difference looks like in practice.

**Vague prompt**:
> Summarize this article.

**Efficiency prompt**:
> You are an editorial assistant (Role). I have a 1,500-word article about remote work trends that I need to share with my team (Context). Summarize it (Task) in exactly 3 bullet points, each under 25 words, using plain language — no jargon (Constraints).

The vague prompt might get you something usable. The efficiency prompt will get you something you can paste directly into a Slack channel without editing. That's the difference between "AI helped" and "AI saved me 20 minutes."

Here's a second example — this time for extraction instead of summarization:

**Vague prompt**:
> What are the important things in these meeting notes?

**Efficiency prompt**:
> You are a project coordinator (Role). I have raw notes from a 30-minute team meeting about our school fundraiser (Context). Extract every action item, who's responsible, and the deadline for each (Task). Format the output as a markdown table with columns: Action Item, Owner, Due Date. If a deadline wasn't mentioned, write "TBD" (Constraints).

Notice the pattern: the more specific your constraints, the less editing you do afterward. And less editing is the whole point.

#### Constraint Types That Matter

These are the categories of constraints worth thinking about every time you write an efficiency prompt. You won't always need all of them, but scanning this list before you hit send will save you a round of "that's not what I meant":

- **Length**: "In 3 bullet points," "Under 100 words," "One paragraph"
- **Format**: "As a markdown table," "As a numbered list," "In email format"
- **Audience**: "For a 10-year-old," "For a technical team," "For my boss who skims everything"
- **Tone**: "Casual and friendly," "Professional but warm," "Direct and no-nonsense"
- **Exclusions**: "Don't include any technical terms," "Skip the introduction," "No opinions — just facts"

The goal isn't to use every constraint type on every prompt. It's to have the vocabulary so you can specify exactly what you need when the default output isn't right.

### 5. When NOT to Use AI

So far, this lesson has been about all the things AI can do for you. Now let's talk about when you should keep your hands on the wheel.

Think about it this way: there's a difference between the quality bar for a text message to your friend and the quality bar for a college application. The text message? If it's 90% right, nobody cares. The application? If there's one wrong detail — a fabricated activity, an inaccurate date, a claim you can't back up — it could cost you the acceptance. **The stakes of the output determine whether AI is the right tool.**

Remember hallucination from Lesson 1.1? The AI generates the most *probable* response, not the most *accurate* one. For transformation tasks — where the source material already exists and the AI is just reshaping it — hallucination risk is low. The information is right there in the prompt. But the further you move from transformation toward *creation* and *judgment*, the more risk you take on.

Here's a practical framework. Think about two axes: **how structured the task is** and **how high the stakes are**.

| | Low Stakes | High Stakes |
|---|---|---|
| **Structured** (clear input, clear output) | Great for AI — formatting, summarizing, extracting | Use AI, but verify everything — contracts, reports, applications |
| **Unstructured** (open-ended, ambiguous) | AI can help brainstorm, but expect to curate heavily | Danger zone — original arguments, medical/legal/financial advice, anything where being wrong has consequences |

#### Red Flags: Stop and Think

Not every risky task is obvious. Here are the signals that should make you pause before hitting "send" on a delegation:

- **The output will be taken as fact** (academic citations, legal claims, medical information, financial figures)
- **The task requires your specific lived experience** (personal essays, recommendation letters, sensitive communications)
- **Getting it wrong has real consequences** (job applications, contracts, public-facing communications)
- **You can't verify the output** (if you don't know enough about the topic to check the AI's work, you can't catch its mistakes)

That last one is the sneakiest trap. AI sounds confident whether it's right or wrong — you learned that in Lesson 1.1. If you delegate a task you don't understand to an AI you can't verify, you're not being efficient. You're outsourcing your judgment to a probability engine.

### 6. Measuring the Win

You've delegated a task to AI. It gave you something back. But here's the question nobody asks enough: **was it actually worth it?**

Think of it like a speedrun timer in a video game. Speedrunners don't just play fast — they measure *exactly* how much time each strategy saves and whether the quality of the run holds up. If a shortcut saves 10 seconds but causes you to fail the level 30% of the time, it's not actually a good shortcut.

The same logic applies to AI efficiency. There are three things worth measuring:

#### Time Saved

This one's straightforward. How long would the task have taken you manually? How long did it take with AI (including the time to write the prompt, review the output, and make edits)? If you spent 15 minutes crafting the perfect prompt for a task that would have taken you 10 minutes by hand, that's not efficiency — that's a hobby.

#### Quality Check: Is This Good Enough?

"Good enough" sounds lazy, but it's actually a sophisticated judgment call — and this is the skill that separates someone who uses AI well from someone who just uses AI. Here's a quick checklist to run every time you get output back from AI:

- **Accuracy**: Are the facts, dates, names, and numbers correct? Cross-check anything that would be embarrassing if wrong.
- **Completeness**: Did it cover everything you asked for, or did it quietly skip something? AI often drops the hardest part of a request.
- **Tone and voice**: Does it sound like the right person wrote it? A summary for your study group should sound different from one for a professor.
- **Hallucination check**: Is anything in here that *wasn't* in the source material? If the AI added a claim or a detail you didn't provide, verify it.
- **The "would I sign this?" test**: If someone saw this output with your name on it, would you be comfortable? If not, it needs more editing.

The quality bar changes with the task. A rough summary for your own notes? The bar is low — speed matters more than polish. A summary going into a group presentation? The bar is higher. Knowing *which* quality bar applies to *this* task is part of the efficiency skill.

#### The Attention Dividend

This is the one people miss — and it might be the most important measurement of all. The real win often isn't the time saved on *this* task. It's what you did with the freed-up time and mental energy.

Here's a concrete example. Say you have a group project due Friday. You need to compile everyone's messy notes into a structured outline, format the bibliography, and write the introduction. The compiling and formatting are transformation tasks — AI handles them in 15 minutes instead of the two hours they'd normally take. Now you've got an extra hour and 45 minutes. If you spend that time actually *thinking* about your argument, researching a stronger opening example, or reviewing the logic of your outline — you didn't just do the same project faster. You did a *better* project. The AI didn't improve your thinking. It cleared the runway so your thinking could actually take off.

That's the attention dividend: the difference between "I saved time" and "I invested the saved time in work that actually needed my brain." Over a semester, over a career, that compounds. The people who get the most out of AI aren't the ones who automate the most tasks — they're the ones who redirect the freed-up attention to the work that matters most.

> The goal of AI efficiency isn't to do more tasks per hour. It's to spend more of your hours on tasks that actually need *you*.

Getting in the habit of measuring — even informally — is what separates someone who "uses AI sometimes" from someone who has genuinely integrated it into how they work. Over time, you'll develop an intuition for which tasks are worth delegating and which ones aren't. But that intuition starts with paying attention to the results.

And what about when the 0.8 draft isn't good enough? When the AI's first attempt misses the mark and you need to push it further? That's not a failure of the approach — it's the beginning of a different skill entirely. We'll get into that next lesson.

## Activities

1. **The Format Flip**: Find or create a block of unformatted text — a paragraph that contains names, dates, numbers, and action items all jumbled together. (You can use a real meeting recap, class notes, or just make one up.) Write your prompt using the full Role/Context/Task + Constraints framework — label each part before you send it. Then prompt AI to restructure the text three different ways: as a clean markdown table, as a bulleted list, and as a calendar-style schedule. Same information, three shapes. Notice how little you had to change about the content — and how much the format changes the usability.

2. **The Delegation Decision**: Write out a to-do list of 8-10 tasks (use a real one or invent a realistic one — things like "write a thank-you email," "proofread my essay," "decide what to have for dinner," "create a study schedule," "write my personal statement"). For each task, decide: AI-appropriate or human-only? Write a one-sentence justification for each, referencing the sous-chef model or the stakes/structure matrix. The goal isn't to get them all "right" — it's to practice thinking through *why* a task does or doesn't fit.

3. **Speedrun Challenge**: Pick a real task that involves transformation — summarize an article, format some messy data, or extract key points from a long document. Time yourself doing it completely by hand. Then do the same task with AI (including prompt-writing and editing time). Compare: How much time did you save? Was the quality the same, better, or worse? Run the output through the quality checklist from Section 6. Would you delegate this task again?

4. **Spot the Weakness**: Give AI a task from the "danger zone" — something unstructured and open-ended. Try "Write an original argument for why [pick a topic you know well]" or "Give me 5 academic sources about [a niche topic you can verify]." Don't just read the output — evaluate it using the quality checklist. Where is it confident but wrong? Where does it sound good but say nothing? This is your hallucination radar in action.

5. **Prompt Makeover**: Find a vague, one-line prompt for an efficiency task (or write a bad one on purpose — something like "fix my notes"). Rewrite it using the full Role/Context/Task + Constraints framework. Run both the vague version and the rewritten version through AI with the same source material. Compare the two outputs side by side: How much more usable is the constrained version? How much less editing does it need?

## Assignment: The Transformation Showcase

Find a real piece of messy, unstructured text from your own life — meeting notes, a long email thread, class notes, a group chat recap, or anything that's information-dense but poorly organized. It should be at least 300 words of raw material.

Use AI to transform it into **two structured outputs**:

1. **A concise summary** with exactly 3 key takeaways (use the Role/Context/Task + Constraints framework to write your prompt — label each part: Role, Context, Task, Constraints)
2. **A formatted table** that extracts specific information — action items, key people, dates, decisions, or whatever categories make sense for your source material

Then write a **short reflection** (100-200 words) that answers:

- How long would this have taken you manually?
- Is the AI version better, worse, or different than what you'd have produced?
- What did you have to fix or adjust in the AI's output? (Use the quality checklist: accuracy, completeness, tone, hallucination check)
- Based on the stakes-and-structure framework, was this a good task to delegate? Why?

Submit your original source text, both AI outputs, the prompts you used (with R/C/T/C parts labeled), and your reflection.

**Check**: Use the Assessment tool to verify your work.
