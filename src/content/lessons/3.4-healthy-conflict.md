## Introduction

Conflict is inevitable — in open source communities, remote teams, and client relationships alike. The goal is not to avoid it, but to navigate it in ways that preserve trust, surface real problems, and lead to better outcomes for everyone involved. Healthy conflict is not about winning. It is about understanding.

## Enduring Understandings

1. Most people are not angry at you. They are angry at feeling ignored, uninformed, or unimportant.
2. Uncertainty is more damaging than bad news — a clear "no with a path" builds more trust than a vague "I'm working on it".
3. Every conflict has at least two perspectives. Understanding both is what makes resolution possible.
4. Before you can solve anything, you need to understand what the person actually cannot do or is angry about. Calmly ask before jumping to conclusions.
5. Delivering a difficult truth professionally is a skill that requires preparation, courage, and empathy in equal measure.
6. People remember how you made them feel in a difficult moment far longer than they remember the outcome.

## Essential Questions

1. Do I know what made this person frustrated? Do I understand their problem well enough to explain it back to them before I respond?
2. How do I say something negative in a way that makes this person respect me more, not less?
3. Has this person felt understood before I moved to explaining or solving?
4. What does the situation look like from the other side?

## Activities

### 1. Conflict Type Mapping
Learn to identify conflict layers and why each calls for a different response.

### 2. WHY The Chicken Crossed The Road
Practice setting context and diagnosing real needs through structured questioning before jumping to solutions.

### 3. I've Looked at Life from Both Sides Now
Analyze real conflicts from both perspectives simultaneously to practice empathy.

### 4. Curated Transparency
Case studies on how much to share, when, and with whom.

### 5. The "No With a Path" Framework
Practice turning a negative into a constructive, trust-building response using real WordPress community scenarios.

## Assessment

Think of a real conflict you have experienced or witnessed — in a professional, academic, or open source context. It could be a PR rejection with no clear feedback, a scope disagreement, a missed deadline, or any situation where tension arose between people with different needs or expectations.

Write a structured reflection in three parts:

**Diagnose:** Describe the conflict. What was the stated problem? Try to identify the layers of frustration. What was the real underlying problem? What questions would you ask — or wish someone had asked — to get to that real problem faster?

**Respond:** Write two responses to the conflict, one from each side. For example, if your scenario is a rejected PR, write the contributor's response to the rejection and the maintainer's response if they had communicated the rejection clearly and constructively from the start. Show how a "no with a path" looks from both perspectives.

**Reflect:** What made this conflict hard? What would have made it easier?

## Mastery Criteria

1. Correctly identifies the difference between the stated problem and the underlying need.
2. Asks diagnostic questions in a logical sequence before jumping to a response.
3. Delivers the difficult truth clearly (no false hope, no avoidance).
4. Demonstrates empathy before solution in both responses.
5. Reflection is honest and specific, and is not defensive.
6. Tone is professional and constructive, even when the answer is "no".

## UDL Accommodations

**Engagement:** Learners bring their own real conflict situation, making the assessment immediately relevant and personally meaningful. Any real life scenario is valid — a misread Slack message counts, as well as a project disagreement or a rejected PR.

**Representation:** The conflict framework is presented as a written guide and a visual decision flowchart showing the "diagnose > respond > reflect" sequence.

**Action:** The assessment is written, removing the pressure of live performance for learners who are new to conflict or shy about it.
